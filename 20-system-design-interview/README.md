# System Design Interview

**→ [The question bank](QUESTIONS.md)** — 46 questions across 9 tracks, each with an escalating
follow-up chain.

An interview is not a design exercise with an audience. It is a *conversation about trade-offs*
conducted under a clock, and the thing being scored is almost never the architecture you end up
with — two candidates can draw the identical diagram and only one of them passes. What separates
them is whether each box arrived because a bottleneck forced it, and whether they said out loud
what it cost.

Three failure modes account for most of it, and none is a knowledge gap:

- **Drawing before defining.** Boxes in the first five minutes means designing for a problem you
  have not stated. Restate the problem, get three functional requirements agreed, and defer the
  rest out loud.
- **Components with no bottleneck attached.** A cache nobody justified is worth less than no cache,
  because it tells the interviewer you add things by reflex. Name the bottleneck, make one change,
  name what it cost.
- **No numbers.** *"It scales"* is not a claim. One division — the read:write ratio — changes more
  design decisions than any other sentence you can say.

---

## The four approaches, by clock

The same method at four resolutions. Pick by how long you have, not by how much you know.

| You have | Do this | The one thing you must produce |
|---|---|---|
| **5 min** | Requirements and one number. Restate the problem, name three functional requirements, state the latency/availability/consistency targets, and compute peak rps and the read:write ratio. | A number that **rules something out** |
| **15 min** | The above, plus the simple version and its ceiling. Client → server → database, then say *"this is correct up to roughly N rps"* and identify what saturates first. | A named **bottleneck**, not a diagram |
| **30 min** | The above, plus scaling that bottleneck and the two it forces. Each change: bottleneck → one change → what it cost. Then failure: what happens when each component dies. | The **chain**, with costs stated |
| **45 min** | The full [design checklist](../DESIGN-CHECKLIST.md) — 5 min requirements, 5 sizing, 5 APIs and data, 5 the simple version, 15 scaling, 5 failure, 5 finish. | Three **trade-offs stated unprompted** |

Two things are true at every duration. The order never changes — requirements, numbers, simple
version, bottleneck, scale, failure — and **you say the cost of every component as you add it**. A
candidate who runs out of time having done the first three steps properly interviews far better
than one who reached a finished diagram by skipping them.

The 45-minute column is the only one where you should expect to finish. The others are not
shortened designs; they are the same design, stopped honestly at a defensible point.

## What is actually being scored

The four questions at the end of the [design checklist](../DESIGN-CHECKLIST.md#the-four-questions-that-expose-depth)
are the real rubric, and they are worth rehearsing on any system you already know:

1. What did each component **cost** you?
2. What breaks first as traffic grows 10x?
3. Where would you accept data loss, and where never?
4. What is the **simplest** thing you could have built instead, and why isn't it enough?

Question 4 is the one candidates are least prepared for and it carries the most signal. **If your
options table has no row for "do nothing", you have not finished thinking.**

## How to use the question bank

[QUESTIONS.md](QUESTIONS.md) is built for retrieval practice rather than reading. Every question
carries a `What they are really asking` line, which matters more than the answer — most questions
have a surface reading and an actual one, and answering the surface reading fluently is still the
wrong answer. Answers are folded; say yours out loud first.

The **follow-ups are the real test**. Anyone can recite a first answer, and your interviewer has
heard it before. Each follow-up is the question that falls out of the previous *answer*, which is
exactly how a real interviewer digs — if your first answer cannot survive being asked *"and then
what breaks?"* twice, it was recall rather than understanding.

At least one question per track is a **judgement question whose strong answer is "no"**. Adding a
cache to uniformly random reads, sharding at 3 TB, splitting into microservices before naming a
bottleneck: the candidate who designs the thing they were handed has failed it.

`QUESTIONS.md` and `questions.json` are generated by
[`scripts/gen_interview.py`](../scripts/gen_interview.py) from
[`scripts/interview_data.py`](../scripts/interview_data.py); CI fails if the committed files drift
from the data. Edit the data, not the markdown.
